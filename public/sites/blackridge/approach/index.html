The approach

Approach

Four principles, applied slowly. Less a method than a set of promises we make to each site.

Four principles One standing rule
01

Site before sketch

No drawing until the ground has been walked at the hours the building will be used: the 7am frost, the 4pm glare, the sound of the road after rain. We survey the light like a client and the wind like a sailor. The plan that follows is a record of that walking, which is why our houses rarely sit the way newcomers expect. They sit the way the site says.

It is also why we take few commissions. A proper first season (two visits, a camp, a tide chart) costs more time than most practices can give. We think it is the cheapest thing on the bill.

02

A palette drawn from the ground

Every project gets its materials from its map wherever the budget honestly allows: basalt at Omaha, rammed site-soil in Central Otago, kept brick in Auckland. What must be brought in is chosen to age toward the site, cedar that silvers, copper that dulls, limestone that darkens at the drip line. A building that will not weather gracefully has not yet been designed, only styled.

The palette is deliberately short. Three or four materials, detailed well, outperform ten materials detailed adequately. A short palette is also a quieter one.

03

Craft you can touch

Where the hand meets the building, the screen rail, the bench nose, the stair apron, the door pull, is where architecture is actually experienced. We detail those moments with the makers who will build them: the joiner who will cut the screens sits in the drawing set's second meeting, not the last. Site workshops beat site instructions.

We keep a model shop in the studio and use it constantly. If a junction cannot be built in card at 1:20, it will not survive contact with a real site at 1:1.

04

Built to age in place

Sustainability, for us, is mostly a noun: the building should still be there, loved, in eighty years. That means plans simple enough to change hands, materials that can be repaired rather than replaced, and thermal mass and shading resolved before any machine is counted on. The greenest building is the one that never needs demolishing.

We measure success on a longer clock than handover. Do the owners still open the screens at dawn in winter? Does the guesthouse courtyard still gather the evening cool? We go back and check. Good buildings settle into their ground and get on with it.

Try the study

One pad, one room, one roof. The whole method in a model.

Our standing study model, a hill pavilion, sits on the homepage as an interactive drawing. Site, Structure and Light are exactly the three questions we ask of every commission before the first plan is drawn.
