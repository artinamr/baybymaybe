The second decade

April 2026· 6 min read· Words: the studio

A building is finished on the day it opens. It is not done for another twenty years.

There is a photograph we keep pinned above the drawing table. It was taken eleven years after we finished a small house in the Māniototo, and almost nothing in it matches the drawings. The cedar has gone the colour of the tussock behind it. The client's divergent rose has walked six metres along the veranda rail. A population of lichen has claimed the south elevation, and the door closer sticks in frost, exactly as the builder warned it would. It is the best building we have made, and it does not look like ours anymore. It looks like theirs. That is the point.

Architecture has an odd relationship with time. The magazines photograph a house twice: the week it is finished, and again when its architect dies. Everything in between, the two decades when the building is actually being lived in, argued with, repaired, extended and forgiven, goes unrecorded. We think that middle stretch is the work. Handover photographs show what a client bought. The second decade shows what they got.

So we design for it deliberately. Materials are chosen not for the day they are installed but for the day they stop being new. Blackened cedar is specified because of how it silvers, not how it photographs. Limestone render is left to darken where the falls run, so the wall keeps a wet-weather map of its own roof. Copper is left mill-finish to walk from penny-bright to the green of old church roofs at its own pace. Where a material cannot age honestly, we say so to the client before they fall in love with it. Paint fails; so does pretence.

We draw the building at handover, and then we draw it again at thirty, when the vine has taken the west wall and nobody remembers which walls were load-bearing.

Studio maxim, written on no whiteboard that survives

Joints get the same pessimism. A flashing detailed for its first winter is a different object from one detailed for its fifteenth, when the sealant has hardened, the painter has been heavy-handed, and a golf ball has hit the corner cladding twice. We detail the tenth year: access for a ladder, a sacrificial board where the trampoline will eventually strike, fixings a contractor can find without a drawing. Weathering is not the absence of maintenance. It is maintenance made predictable.

The other thing that ages a building is its garden, and we have learned to treat planting as structure. A row of grapes on a west screen will do more for a summer living room than any shading device we can draw; a hedge takes a decade to become a windbreak, so the hedge goes in first, before the concrete trucks, if the client can bear the wait. Buildings that settle are buildings whose edges were designed to be argued with. Leave a wall for the vine. Leave a wall for the paintings that have not been painted yet.

Since 2019 we have run what we call second-decade reviews. Ten years after handover, we go back with a camera, a moisture meter and a thermos, and we stay a night if the clients will have us. We are looking for the honest failures: the door that was never in the right place, the shelf the sun bleaches every February, the window seat that turned out to be the only seat anyone uses. Some of what we learn is humbling. All of it is in the current drawings.

Clients occasionally ask why we seem more interested in year fifteen than in opening day. The answer is the one the lichen taught us: a building belongs to its place when the place has had time to edit it. Our job is to draw the first draft well enough, and loosely enough, that the edits make it better.
